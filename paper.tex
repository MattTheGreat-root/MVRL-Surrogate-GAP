\begin{abstract}
\noindent
Reinforcement learning applied to multi-period asset allocation typically encodes
risk into a per-period reward---a Sharpe ratio, a variance penalty---and maximises
the sum of those rewards. The resulting objective is not the mean-variance
criterion it is meant to represent. That the two formulations differ is known in
the portfolio-choice literature; what is quantified here is the map between them.

For an i.i.d.\ market and the variance-penalised reward $r_t=d_t-\kappa d_t^2$,
with $d_t$ the wealth increment, we show that the maximiser of the additive
objective solves a backward recursion whose multi-asset form reduces exactly to a
scalar one through $\nu=m^{\top}M^{-1}m$. Using this we prove that when the gross
riskless return is $1$, the additive surrogate reproduces the equilibrium
mean-variance policy \emph{exactly}, at
\[
\kappa^{\star}=\varphi(1-\nu),
\]
and not at $\kappa=\varphi$. We further prove that for a one-period horizon the
two policies can always be matched, so any irreducible discrepancy is a
multi-period phenomenon; and that for a two-period horizon with a non-unit
riskless return the minimal attainable policy distance is
$c\,|s-1|+O((s-1)^2)$ with $c$ in closed form. Numerically the linear rate
persists at longer horizons.

We place these results inside an exact decomposition of the shortfall of a
trained agent against the pre-committed optimum into three terms---the price of
time-consistency, the surrogate gap, and the learning error---and measure all
three. Which term dominates depends on the risk aversion, with a crossover in our
market near $\varphi=12$. Finally, we exhibit configurations in which the Sharpe
ratio ranks two policies in the opposite order to the mean-variance objective,
so that backtest comparison cannot detect the effect of reward design.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Reinforcement learning is now routinely applied to multi-period asset allocation.
The surveys of \citet{bai} and \citet{hambly} between them cover several dozen
papers in this area, and across both a consistent construction appears.
The practitioner wants a policy trading return against risk, encodes risk into a
per-period reward---portfolio return, a Sharpe ratio, a differential Sharpe ratio,
a variance penalty---and maximises the sum of those rewards with a standard
solver.

The objective so constructed is not the mean-variance criterion. It is a sum,
therefore time-consistent, therefore amenable to dynamic programming; the
mean-variance criterion is none of these. That the two formulations differ is
not new. \citet{garleanu} and \citet{demiguel} optimise a discounted sum of
period-by-period mean-variance tradeoffs, while \citet{ling} extend Markowitz to
the mean and variance of terminal wealth; the distinction is standard in that
literature. What appears not to have been done is to quantify the map between
them, and in particular to say what a practitioner choosing a per-period risk
penalty is implicitly choosing.

That is the question here. Section~\ref{sec:recursion} derives the maximiser of
the additive objective for the variance-penalised reward. Section~\ref{sec:main}
proves that at a unit gross riskless return the additive surrogate reproduces the
equilibrium mean-variance policy exactly, at $\kappa^{\star}=\varphi(1-\nu)$
where $\nu=m^{\top}M^{-1}m$; that a one-period horizon always admits an exact
match; and that at two periods with $s\neq1$ the minimal attainable distance is
linear in $s-1$ with a closed-form constant. Section~\ref{sec:numerics} places
these inside an exact three-term decomposition of the shortfall of a trained
agent and measures all three terms.

The practical content is short enough to state here. A practitioner who sets the
reward's risk penalty equal to their risk aversion is making an error whose size
is computable: the correct value is $\varphi(1-\nu)$, which in our demonstration
market is $0.806$ rather than $1$. And the error is invisible to the evaluation
the field uses, because we exhibit policies that the Sharpe ratio ranks in the
opposite order to the objective.

\section{Related work}\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Multiperiod mean-variance.} \citet{ling} solve the terminal-wealth
problem by embedding it in an auxiliary linear-quadratic problem, giving a
pre-committed policy affine in wealth; \citet{zhouli} give the continuous-time
counterpart. A parallel strand studies time-consistent policies:
\citet{strotz} identifies the phenomenon, \citet{basak} characterise the
equilibrium mean-variance policy, \citet{bjork} give the general Markovian
theory in discrete time, and \citet{bjorkzhou} extend it to state-dependent risk
aversion, noting that the constant-risk-aversion equilibrium invests a dollar
amount independent of wealth---the property our Theorem~\ref{thm:main} proof
establishes in discrete time as $A_t^2=B_t$. Our Section~\ref{sec:main} works entirely inside this
framework and adds nothing to it; the two policies are inputs to our comparison,
not outputs.

\paragraph{Summed per-period objectives.} A separate line optimises a discounted
sum of single-period mean-variance tradeoffs rather than a terminal criterion.
\citet{garleanu} do this with quadratic transaction costs, obtaining the
aim-portfolio characterisation; \citet{demiguel} work in a related setting. That
this formulation differs from \citet{ling} is understood in that literature and
we claim no novelty for the observation. Our contribution is the quantitative
map: Theorem~\ref{thm:main} says which per-period penalty corresponds to which
terminal risk aversion, and Theorem~\ref{thm:t2} bounds the residual when no
exact correspondence exists.

\paragraph{Reinforcement learning in finance.} \citet{bai} survey the applied
literature and classify the rewards in use, of which volatility-adjusted return
is a principal category. \citet{hambly} cover the same ground from the
mathematical side and note that the mean-variance framework is time-inconsistent
so that the Bellman equation fails, and separately that convergence guarantees
for the deterministic policy gradient family were unavailable at the time of
writing; \citet{xiong} have since established convergence of DPG to a stationary
policy. \citet{wang} treat exploration for the continuous-time mean-variance
problem correctly, via entropy regularisation, obtaining a Gaussian optimal
exploratory policy. None of this work asks what the discrete-time additive
surrogate optimises, which is our question.

\paragraph{Reward design.} The general problem of an optimised proxy diverging
from the intended objective is familiar in reinforcement learning, usually under
the heading of reward misspecification or reward hacking. The instance studied
here is unusually tractable: both the intended objective and the surrogate admit
closed-form solutions, so the discrepancy can be computed rather than estimated,
and the mechanism---failure of the tower property for the variance---is
identifiable rather than emergent.

\section{Setting}\label{sec:setting}

Let there be $n$ risky assets and a horizon $T$. Following \citet{hambly}, itself
following \citet{ling}, write $s$ for the gross riskless return per period and
$P_t\in\R^{n}$ for the excess gross returns, independent across $t$ with
\begin{equation}
m=\E[P_t],\qquad M=\E[P_tP_t^{\top}]=\Sigma+mm^{\top},\qquad
\nu=m^{\top}M^{-1}m .
\end{equation}
We assume $M\succ0$, which gives $\nu\in[0,1)$. Write $d=M^{-1}m$ for the
direction that will appear throughout. With $u_t\in\R^{n}$ the vector of amounts
held in the risky assets and $x_t$ wealth,
\begin{equation}
x_{t+1}=s\,x_t+P_t^{\top}u_t ,\qquad d_t:=x_{t+1}-x_t=(s-1)x_t+P_t^{\top}u_t .
\label{eq:dynamics}
\end{equation}
The mean-variance criterion evaluated from time $t$ at wealth $x$ is
\begin{equation}
J(t,x,u)=\E_{t,x}[x_T]-\varphi\Var_{t,x}(x_T),\qquad \varphi>0,
\label{eq:mv}
\end{equation}
and we write $J(\pi)=J(0,x_0,\pi)$.

\subsection{Three notions of optimality}

Because \eqref{eq:mv} contains the square of a conditional expectation, it does
not satisfy the tower property and the Bellman equation fails
\citep{strotz,bjork}. Three inequivalent objects result.

The \emph{pre-committed} policy maximises \eqref{eq:mv} at time $0$ and is
executed without revision. \citet{ling} obtain it by embedding in an auxiliary
linear-quadratic problem; it is affine in wealth, $u^{\star}_t(x)=\alpha_tx+\beta_t$.

The \emph{equilibrium} policy $\pieq$ is the consistent-planning solution: for all
$t,x$ and admissible $a$,
\begin{equation}
J(t,x,\pieq)\;\ge\;J\bigl(t,x,(a,\pieq_{t+1:T})\bigr).
\label{eq:equilibrium}
\end{equation}
\citet{basak} characterise it and show it differs from the pre-committed policy.

The \emph{myopic} policy applies the single-period optimiser repeatedly.

\subsection{The additive surrogate}

Fix a per-period reward $r_t$ and let
\begin{equation}
J_r(\pi)=\E\Bigl[\textstyle\sum_{t}r_t\Bigr],\qquad
\pir=\arg\max_{\pi}J_r(\pi).
\end{equation}
We study the variance-penalised family
\begin{equation}
r_t=d_t-\kappa\,d_t^{2},\qquad \kappa>0 ,
\label{eq:reward}
\end{equation}
which is the additive stand-in for \eqref{eq:mv} that practitioners write down.

\begin{remark}[the plain-return reward]
With $\kappa=0$ the reward telescopes to $x_T-x_0$ and $J_r$ rewards unbounded
leverage, so no maximiser exists. The mean-variance problem it stands in for is
bounded and solvable. This is elementary but worth stating, because
\citet{bai} report plain portfolio return as the most common reward in the
surveyed literature. An algorithm run on it does not solve a poorly conditioned
version of \eqref{eq:mv}; it solves a problem with no solution, and what it
returns is determined by leverage limits and stopping rules.
\end{remark}

\section{The surrogate optimum}\label{sec:recursion}

\begin{proposition}[Backward recursion]\label{prop:recursion}
For the reward \eqref{eq:reward}, the maximiser of $J_r$ over affine policies is
$\pir_t(x)=\alpha_tx+\beta_t$ with
\begin{align}
\alpha_t&=-\,d\;\frac{g_t}{\kappa-p_{t+1}}, &
\beta_t&=\;\;d\;\frac{1+q_{t+1}}{2(\kappa-p_{t+1})},
\label{eq:policy}\\[0.3em]
p_t&=\frac{\kappa p_{t+1}-(1-\nu)g_t^{2}}{\kappa-p_{t+1}}, &
1+q_t&=(1+q_{t+1})\Bigl(s-\frac{\nu g_t}{\kappa-p_{t+1}}\Bigr),
\label{eq:coeffs}
\end{align}
where $g_t=\kappa(s-1)-p_{t+1}s$, started from $p_T=q_T=0$. The one-step problem
is concave at every step, so the recursion is well defined for every
$\kappa>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{lemma}[Automatic concavity]\label{lem:concave}
For every $\kappa>0$ and every $t$, $p_t\le0$, hence $\kappa-p_{t+1}\ge\kappa>0$.
\end{lemma}

\begin{proof}
Backward induction. $p_T=0$. If $p_{t+1}\le0$ then the numerator of $p_t$ in
\eqref{eq:coeffs} is $\kappa p_{t+1}-(1-\nu)g_t^{2}\le0$, since $\kappa>0$ and
$\nu<1$, while the denominator is $\kappa-p_{t+1}\ge\kappa>0$. Hence $p_t\le0$.
\end{proof}

\begin{proof}
$J_r$ is a sum of per-period terms, so the dynamic programming principle applies.
Let $W_t(x)=p_tx^2+q_tx+c_t$ with $W_T\equiv0$. Using \eqref{eq:dynamics} and
independence of $P_t$ from $x_t$,
\[
\E\bigl[r_t+W_{t+1}(x_{t+1})\bigr]
=(s-1)x+m^{\top}u-\kappa\bigl[(s-1)^2x^2+2(s-1)x\,m^{\top}u+u^{\top}Mu\bigr]
\]
\[
\qquad+\;p_{t+1}\bigl[s^2x^2+2sx\,m^{\top}u+u^{\top}Mu\bigr]
+q_{t+1}\bigl[sx+m^{\top}u\bigr]+c_{t+1}.
\]
The gradient in $u$ is
$m(1+q_{t+1})-2\kappa(s-1)x\,m+2p_{t+1}sx\,m+2(p_{t+1}-\kappa)Mu$, and the
Hessian is $2(p_{t+1}-\kappa)M$, negative definite exactly when
$\kappa>p_{t+1}$, which Lemma~\ref{lem:concave} supplies. Setting the gradient
to zero and solving gives
\eqref{eq:policy}. Substituting back and collecting powers of $x$ gives
\eqref{eq:coeffs}.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}[multi-asset reduces to scalar]\label{rem:scalar}
Every asset-dependent quantity enters \eqref{eq:policy}--\eqref{eq:coeffs} only
through the scalar $\nu$ and the fixed direction $d$: with $u=dz$ one has
$m^{\top}u=\nu z$ and $u^{\top}Mu=\nu z^{2}$. The $n$-asset problem is therefore
the one-asset problem with $\nu=m^{\top}M^{-1}m$. All results below are stated
for general $n$ at no extra cost.
\end{remark}

\section{Main results}\label{sec:main}

\begin{theorem}[Exact recovery at a unit riskless return]\label{thm:main}
Let $s=1$. Then $p_t=q_t=0$ and $\alpha_t=0$ for all $t$, and
\[
\pir=\pieq
\qquad\Longleftrightarrow\qquad
\kappa=\kappa^{\star}:=\varphi\,(1-\nu).
\]
The identity holds for every horizon $T\ge1$ and every $n\ge1$.
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}
\emph{Surrogate side.} With $s=1$, $g_t=-p_{t+1}$. We show $p_t=0$ by backward
induction. $p_T=0$. If $p_{t+1}=0$ then $g_t=0$, so by \eqref{eq:coeffs}
$p_t=(0-0)/\kappa=0$ and by \eqref{eq:policy} $\alpha_t=0$. Then
$1+q_t=(1+q_{t+1})(1-0)=1+q_{t+1}$, so $q_t=q_T=0$, and
$\beta_t=d/(2\kappa)$ for every $t$.

\emph{Equilibrium side.} Write the moment functions generated by the equilibrium
continuation as $f(t,x)=\E_{t,x}[x_T]=A_tx+a_t$ and
$g(t,x)=\E_{t,x}[x_T^2]=B_tx^2+b_tx+c_t$. The one-step equilibrium condition
\eqref{eq:equilibrium} gives, by the same computation as
Proposition~\ref{prop:recursion} applied to $f-\varphi(g-f^2)$,
\begin{equation}
\alpha^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=s\,(A_{t+1}^2-B_{t+1})\,D_{t+1}^{-1}m,\qquad
\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=\frac{A_{t+1}-\varphi b_{t+1}+2\varphi A_{t+1}a_{t+1}}{2\varphi}
D_{t+1}^{-1}m,
\label{eq:eqpolicy}
\end{equation}
with $D_{t+1}=B_{t+1}M-A_{t+1}^2mm^{\top}$ and $A_T=B_T=1$, $a_T=b_T=0$.
We claim $A_t^2=B_t$ for all $t$, hence $\alpha^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=0$. At $t=T$ this
holds. If $A_{t+1}^2=B_{t+1}$ then $\alpha^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=0$, and propagating the
moment functions under a policy with zero wealth-slope gives $A_t=sA_{t+1}$ and
$B_t=s^2B_{t+1}$, so $A_t^2=s^2A_{t+1}^2=s^2B_{t+1}=B_t$.

With $s=1$ this yields $A_t=B_t=1$. Then
$D_{t+1}=M-mm^{\top}$, and by the Sherman--Morrison identity
\[
(M-mm^{\top})^{-1}m=\frac{M^{-1}m}{1-m^{\top}M^{-1}m}=\frac{d}{1-\nu}.
\]
Propagating the moment functions under $\alpha^{\mathrm{eq}}=0$ gives
$a_t=m^{\top}\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_t+a_{t+1}$ and
$b_t=2m^{\top}\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_t+b_{t+1}$, so $b_t=2a_t$ for all $t$ since both
start at zero. The numerator of $\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_t$ in \eqref{eq:eqpolicy} is
therefore $1-2\varphi a_{t+1}+2\varphi a_{t+1}=1$, giving
\[
\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=\frac{d}{2\varphi(1-\nu)} .
\]

\emph{Matching.} Both policies have zero wealth-slope and constant offset, so
they coincide if and only if $d/(2\kappa)=d/(2\varphi(1-\nu))$, that is
$\kappa=\varphi(1-\nu)$.
\end{proof}

\begin{corollary}[The natural choice is wrong]
Setting the reward's risk penalty equal to the risk aversion, $\kappa=\varphi$,
does not recover the equilibrium policy unless $\nu=0$, that is unless the risky
assets carry no excess return. The relative error in the penalty is $\nu$.
\end{corollary}

\subsection{The correction is a discrete-time effect}\label{sec:ct}

The factor $1-\nu$ separating $\kappa^{\star}$ from $\varphi$ deserves comment,
because it explains both why the result is useful and why it is not visible in
the literature it borders on.

Let the rebalancing interval be $\Delta$. Then $m=\mathcal{O}(\Delta)$ while
$M=\Sigma\Delta+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})$, so
\begin{equation}
\nu=m^{\top}M^{-1}m=\bigl(\mu^{\top}\Sigma^{-1}\mu\bigr)\,\Delta+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2})
=\mathrm{SR}^{2}\Delta+\mathcal{O}(\Delta^{2}),
\label{eq:nuscaling}
\end{equation}
with $\mathrm{SR}$ the annualised Sharpe ratio of the tangency portfolio. The
correction therefore vanishes in the continuous-time limit, and
$\kappa^{\star}\to\varphi$.

Two consequences. First, the equilibrium mean-variance literature works in
continuous time---\citet{basak}, \citet{bjorkzhou}, \citet{wang}---where this
factor is identically zero, so its absence there is expected rather than
surprising. Second, reinforcement learning does not operate in that limit: it
operates at whatever rebalancing frequency the environment is discretised to.
Table~\ref{tab:freq} gives the size of the effect.

\begin{table}[h]\centering\small
\begin{tabular}{lrrr}
\toprule
rebalancing & $\Delta$ (years) & $\nu$ & $\kappa^{\star}/\varphi$\\
\midrule
annual & $1$ & $0.1000$ & $0.900$\\
quarterly & $0.25$ & $0.0270$ & $0.973$\\
monthly & $1/12$ & $0.0092$ & $0.991$\\
weekly & $1/52$ & $0.0021$ & $0.998$\\
daily & $1/252$ & $0.0004$ & $1.000$\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{Size of the correction against rebalancing frequency, for
$\mu=0.06$, $\sigma=0.18$. It is an eleven per cent effect at annual
rebalancing and negligible below weekly.}
\label{tab:freq}
\end{table}

\noindent
So the result matters for coarse rebalancing and not for fine, and
\eqref{eq:nuscaling} says precisely where the boundary lies. A practitioner
rebalancing daily may set $\kappa=\varphi$ with a clear conscience; one
rebalancing annually should not.

\begin{proposition}[One period is always matchable]\label{prop:t1}
Let $T=1$ and let $s>0$ be arbitrary. Then there exists $\kappa>0$ with
$\pir=\pieq$, provided $(s-1)x_0+\bigl(2\varphi(1-\nu)\bigr)^{-1}>0$.
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
With $T=1$, $p_1=q_1=0$ and $g_0=\kappa(s-1)$, so by \eqref{eq:policy}
$\alpha_0=-d(s-1)$ and $\beta_0=d/(2\kappa)$. On the equilibrium side
$A_1=B_1=1$ gives $\alpha^{\mathrm{eq}}_0=0$ and
$\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_0=d/(2\varphi(1-\nu))$. Wealth at time $0$ is the constant
$x_0$, so the two controls agree iff
\[
-(s-1)x_0+\frac{1}{2\kappa}=\frac{1}{2\varphi(1-\nu)},
\]
which is solved by $\kappa^{-1}=2(s-1)x_0+\bigl(\varphi(1-\nu)\bigr)^{-1}$.
\end{proof}

\begin{remark}
Proposition~\ref{prop:t1} isolates the mechanism. At one period the mismatch in
the wealth-slope multiplies a deterministic quantity and is absorbed into the
offset, which the single free parameter $\kappa$ can then correct. With $T\ge2$
the slope multiplies a random wealth whose distribution differs across periods,
and one scalar cannot correct $T$ separate mismatches. The surrogate gap is a
multi-period phenomenon.
\end{remark}

\begin{theorem}[Two periods: linear irreducible gap]\label{thm:t2}
Let $T=2$ and $s=1+\epsilon$. Measure the distance between policies by
\begin{equation}
\|\pi-\pi'\|^{2}=\sum_{t=0}^{T-1}\E\bigl\|\pi_t(x_t)-\pi'_t(x_t)\bigr\|^{2},
\label{eq:distance}
\end{equation}
the expectation taken under $\pi$. Then
\[
\min_{\kappa>0}\bigl\|\pir-\pieq\bigr\|
\;=\;c\,|\epsilon|+O(\epsilon^{2}),
\qquad
c=\|d\|\,\frac{\sqrt{(2-\nu)(1+\nu)}}{2\varphi(1-\nu)} .
\]
\end{theorem}

\begin{proof}[Proof sketch]
By Proposition~\ref{prop:recursion}, $g_1=\kappa\epsilon$ and
$p_1=-(1-\nu)\kappa\epsilon^{2}=O(\epsilon^{2})$, whence
$g_0=\kappa\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2})$ and
\[
\alpha_0=\alpha_1=-d\,\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}),
\]
independent of $\kappa$ at leading order: the wealth-slope mismatch cannot be
tuned away. Expanding $\beta_t$ and, on the equilibrium side,
$A_t=s^{T-t}$, $B_t=s^{2(T-t)}$, $b_t=2sa_t$, gives
$\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_1=d/(2\varphi(1-\nu))$ and
$\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_0=d/(2\varphi s(1-\nu))$. Substituting into
\eqref{eq:distance}, writing $\kappa=\kappa^{\star}(1+w\epsilon)$ and retaining
terms of order $\epsilon^{2}$ yields a quadratic in $w$; minimising over $w$ and
taking the square root gives the stated $c$.
\end{proof}

\begin{conjecture}[General horizon]\label{conj}
For every $T\ge2$ there is $c_T>0$, depending on $\nu,\varphi,T$ and $\|d\|$,
with $\min_{\kappa}\|\pir-\pieq\|=c_T|s-1|+O((s-1)^2)$.
\end{conjecture}

Section~\ref{sec:numerics} reports fitted exponents of $1.0000$ across every
configuration tested. The dependence of $c_T$ on the horizon is superlinear: a
log--log fit over $T\in\{2,\dots,10\}$ gives an exponent of $1.16$ with visible
curvature and a maximum log residual of $0.08$, so $c_T$ is not a power law in
$T$. We have not obtained it in closed form for $T\ge3$.

Collecting the above, the functional posited in the introduction is
\begin{equation}
\Lambda(\kappa)=\bigl|\kappa-\varphi(1-\nu)\bigr|+c_T\,|s-1| ,
\label{eq:lambda}
\end{equation}
vanishing exactly when $\pir=\pieq$, with the distance bounded linearly in
$\Lambda$. The first term is reward mis-specification, which the practitioner
controls; the second is structural.

\section{Numerical results}\label{sec:numerics}

\subsection{A decomposition}

Let $\pihat$ be the output of a learning algorithm applied to $J_r$. Since $J$ is
a scalar functional of a policy,
\begin{equation}
J(\pipre)-J(\pihat)
=\underbrace{J(\pipre)-J(\pieq)}_{\epsincons}
+\underbrace{J(\pieq)-J(\pir)}_{\epssurr}
+\underbrace{J(\pir)-J(\pihat)}_{\epslearn},
\label{eq:decomp}
\end{equation}
a telescoping identity requiring only that each policy be admissible. The first
term is the price of time-consistency, characterised by \citet{basak}; the third
is optimisation and estimation error, the object of convergence results such as
\citet{xiong}. The middle term is the subject of Section~\ref{sec:main}.

We measure all three on a market of three assets over four periods with
$\mu=(0.06,0.04,0.02)$, volatilities $(0.18,0.12,0.08)$, correlations
$\rho_{12}=0.30$, $\rho_{13}=-0.10$, $\rho_{23}=0.05$, and $s=1.02$, giving
$\nu=0.1943$.

The agent is an affine-Gaussian policy,
$\pi(u\mid x)=\mathcal{N}(\alpha_tx+\beta_t,\sigma_t^2I)$, trained by REINFORCE
with a per-period baseline and Adam. Its policy class contains $\pir$ by
construction; this is deliberate, since otherwise $\epslearn$ would confound
optimisation error with approximation error and the decomposition would not mean
what \eqref{eq:decomp} says. The agent is trained on $J_r$ alone and never
observes $J$.

Hyperparameters: $6000$ iterations, batch $512$, learning rate $0.03$ annealed as
$\sigma^{1/2}$, exploration $\sigma$ decayed geometrically from $0.5$ to $0.03$,
five seeds. One implementation note is worth recording because it is a property
of the estimator rather than of the objective: the score for a Gaussian mean
carries a factor $\sigma^{-2}$, and retaining it makes the gradient variance
diverge as $\sigma$ anneals, so that training peaks early and then degrades. We
absorb it into the learning rate, which is the Fisher preconditioning for this
policy class.

of the terminal distribution and there is no reason for them to agree; the
surprise is only that the field's evaluation practice presumes they do.
Reward-design error cannot be detected by a Sharpe comparison and must be
measured against a known optimum.

\subsection{What follows for practice}

Three recommendations follow from the results above, in decreasing order of
confidence.

First, if a variance-penalised reward is used with the intention of representing
a terminal mean-variance criterion with risk aversion $\varphi$, the penalty
should be set to $\varphi(1-\nu)$ rather than $\varphi$, with
$\nu=m^{\top}M^{-1}m$ estimated from the same data used to train. By
\eqref{eq:nuscaling} this matters when the rebalancing interval is coarse
relative to $\mathrm{SR}^{-2}$ and not otherwise: it is an eleven per cent
correction at annual rebalancing and under a quarter of one per cent at weekly.
Since $\nu$ is a byproduct of estimating the moments the agent needs anyway, the
correction is free.

Second, a plain-return reward should not be used at all in an unconstrained
setting. It has no maximiser, and whatever an algorithm returns is determined by
leverage limits and stopping rules rather than by the objective.

Third, reported Sharpe ratios should not be used to compare policies trained on
different rewards. Table~\ref{tab:sharpe} exhibits a reversal, and the mechanism
is general: the Sharpe ratio and the mean-variance objective are different
functionals of the terminal distribution, and nothing forces them to agree on an
ordering. Where a ground truth is available, as in the synthetic setting used
here, policies should be compared in policy space.

\section{The differential Sharpe ratio}\label{sec:dsr}

The reward class treated so far is the variance-penalised family. Both surveys
report the differential Sharpe ratio of \citet{moody} as a further standard
choice, and it does not obviously belong to that family: it depends on
statistics accumulated along the trajectory and so is not a function of wealth
alone.

Write $\rho_t$ for the period return and let $A_t,B_t$ be exponentially weighted
estimates of its first two moments with decay $\eta$,
\[
A_t=A_{t-1}+\eta(\rho_t-A_{t-1}),\qquad
B_t=B_{t-1}+\eta(\rho_t^{2}-B_{t-1}),
\]
\[
r^{\mathrm{DSR}}_t=\frac{B_{t-1}\Delta A_t-\tfrac12A_{t-1}\Delta B_t}
{(B_{t-1}-A_{t-1}^{2})^{3/2}} .
\]

\begin{proposition}\label{prop:dsr}
Up to a positive state-dependent scale and an additive constant,
\[
r^{\mathrm{DSR}}_t\;\propto\;\rho_t-\kappa^{\mathrm{eff}}_t\rho_t^{2},
\qquad
\kappa^{\mathrm{eff}}_t=\frac{A_{t-1}}{2B_{t-1}} .
\]
\end{proposition}

\begin{proof}
Substituting the increments,
$B\Delta A-\tfrac12A\Delta B=\tfrac{\eta}{2}\bigl(2B\rho-A\rho^{2}-AB\bigr)$.
Dividing by the denominator, which is positive and independent of $\rho_t$,
gives a term linear in $\rho_t$ with coefficient $\eta B(B-A^{2})^{-3/2}$, a
term in $\rho_t^{2}$ with coefficient $-\tfrac{\eta}{2}A(B-A^{2})^{-3/2}$, and a
constant. The ratio of the second to the first is $A/(2B)$.
\end{proof}

So the differential Sharpe ratio is a variance-penalised reward after all. The
distinction is that its penalty is not chosen. Since $A_t$ and $B_t$ are
consistent estimators of the first two moments of the realised return, in the
stationary regime
\begin{equation}
\kappa^{\mathrm{eff}}\;\longrightarrow\;\frac{\E[\rho]}{2\,\E[\rho^{2}]},
\label{eq:kappaeff}
\end{equation}
a quantity containing no preference parameter at all. Numerically, over a
$200$-period horizon the estimates converge to the true moments
($A^{\star}=0.021636$ against $\E[\rho]=0.021676$; $B^{\star}=0.000479$ against
$\E[\rho^{2}]=0.000482$), giving $\kappa^{\mathrm{eff}}=22.51$. Optimising the
differential Sharpe reward directly over affine policies and then searching the
variance-penalised family for the closest member returns $\kappa=21.56$, a
$4\%$ agreement, at a residual policy distance of $0.019$.

Reading Theorem~\ref{thm:main} backwards then gives an implied risk aversion
\begin{equation}
\varphi^{\mathrm{implied}}=\frac{\kappa^{\mathrm{eff}}}{1-\nu}=27.9
\end{equation}
for this market. A practitioner selecting this reward believes they are asking
for risk-adjusted return; they are in fact asking for a mean-variance criterion
at a risk aversion the market sets and nothing in their setup reports. Varying
the decay $\eta$ over $[0.02,0.4]$ moves it only between $30.4$ and $31.0$, so
the effect is not a hyperparameter artefact.

The differential Sharpe ratio is therefore covered by
Theorem~\ref{thm:main} rather than excluded from it, with
$|\kappa^{\mathrm{eff}}-\varphi(1-\nu)|$ as the first term of $\Lambda$. What is
different is that this term cannot be driven to zero by choice.

\section{Out of model}\label{sec:oom}

Every result so far lives inside the i.i.d.\ market where the closed forms
exist. We evaluate the four policies under processes matched to the same first
two moments but violating the model in different ways: Student-$t$ innovations
with four degrees of freedom, GARCH-like volatility clustering, and AR(1) serial
correlation.

\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule

\midrule
Gaussian & $1.4267$ & $1.3236$ & $1.3261$ & $1.3166$\\
Student-$t$ & $1.4252$ & $1.3238$ & $1.3263$ & $1.3166$\\

leave the ranking intact; serial correlation reverses it.}

Fat tails and volatility clustering change $J$ in the third decimal and leave
the ordering untouched, which is expected: they perturb higher moments while
preserving independence across periods, and the analysis uses only the first
two moments. The results are not artefacts of Gaussianity.

Serial correlation is different and the ordering reverses. The pre-committed
policy is worst affected, falling from $1.427$ to $0.929$: its wealth slope is
calibrated to a drift that momentum makes wrong, and by construction it cannot
revise. This is a real limitation rather than a caveat. Every closed form here
assumes independence across periods, and once that fails the reference policies
are no longer the objects they are claimed to be.

\subsection{Statistical power of a backtest}

Section~\ref{sec:numerics} shows the Sharpe ratio ordering these policies
incorrectly. A natural objection is that the difference may be too small to
matter. The question is then whether a finite backtest could detect it.

Drawing $n$ independent replications and asking how often the Sharpe comparison
selects the policy with the better mean-variance objective:

replications & $\Pr(\text{Sharpe selects the }J\text{-better policy})$\\

\section{Limitations and open problems}\label{sec:limits}

\paragraph{Priority.} The distinction between summed per-period mean-variance
tradeoffs and the terminal criterion is standard in portfolio choice
\citep{garleanu,demiguel,ling}. What we believe to be new is the quantitative
map, Theorem~\ref{thm:main}. Searches of the equilibrium mean-variance and
dynamic-trading literatures did not turn it up, and
Section~\ref{sec:ct} offers a reason: the correction is $\mathcal{O}(\Delta)$ and
vanishes in continuous time, which is where that literature works. This is an
argument, not a proof of priority. The author has not conducted a systematic
search and this must be done before the claim is made in a submitted version. If
$\kappa^{\star}=\varphi(1-\nu)$ is known, the contribution reduces to the
decomposition, the transfer to reinforcement learning, and
Section~\ref{sec:numerics}.

\paragraph{Rigour.} Theorem~\ref{thm:t2} is established by series expansion with
the error terms confirmed numerically rather than bounded. Conjecture~\ref{conj}
is supported by fitted exponents only.

\paragraph{Scope.} Returns are i.i.d.\ with known moments; there are no
transaction costs, no short-sale constraints and no leverage limits. Wealth is
unbounded below, so the policies here permit ruin; imposing a no-bankruptcy
constraint would forfeit the closed forms on which the whole comparison rests.

\paragraph{Dependent returns.} Section~\ref{sec:oom} shows the ordering
reversing under AR(1) serial correlation, so the analysis should not be read as
applying to markets with predictability.
Proposition~\ref{prop:recursion} extends directly to time-varying
$(m_t,M_t,s_t)$, with $\nu_t$ replacing $\nu$; Theorem~\ref{thm:main} then
requires $s_t=1$ for all $t$ and $\kappa=\varphi(1-\nu_t)$ simultaneously, which
is generally infeasible for a single $\kappa$. Establishing the size of the
resulting gap is open, and is the most important of the extensions listed here.

\paragraph{On $\epssurr$.} The quantity $\epssurr$ is a difference of scalar
values and is not sign-definite; at $\kappa^{\star}$ in our market it is
$-0.0025$, the surrogate optimum beating the equilibrium policy. Statements of
the form ``$\epssurr=0$ if and only if'' are therefore false and all results here
are stated for the policy distance, with the value gap as a consequence.

\section{Derivation of the constant in Theorem~\ref{thm:t2}}\label{app:t2}

Set $s=1+\epsilon$ and $T=2$. All quantities are expanded to the order needed for
the $\epsilon^{2}$ coefficient of the squared distance.

\paragraph{Surrogate side.} From Proposition~\ref{prop:recursion} with
$p_2=q_2=0$: $g_1=\kappa\epsilon$, so
\[
\alpha_1=-d\,\epsilon,\qquad \beta_1=\frac{d}{2\kappa},\qquad
p_1=-(1-\nu)\kappa\epsilon^{2},\qquad
1+q_1=1+\epsilon(1-\nu)+O(\epsilon^{2}).
\]
Then $g_0=\kappa\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2})$ and $\kappa-p_1=\kappa(1+O(\epsilon^2))$,
so
\[
\alpha_0=-d\,\epsilon+O(\epsilon^{2}),\qquad
\beta_0=\frac{d}{2\kappa}\bigl(1+\epsilon(1-\nu)\bigr)+O(\epsilon^{2}).
\]
The wealth slope is thus $-d\epsilon$ at both dates, independent of $\kappa$ at
leading order. This is the source of the irreducible gap.

\paragraph{Equilibrium side.} From the proof of Theorem~\ref{thm:main},
$\alpha^{\mathrm{eq}}_t=0$, $A_t=s^{T-t}$, $B_t=s^{2(T-t)}$ and $b_t=2sa_t$,
whence $D_{t+1}=s^{2(T-t-1)}(M-mm^{\top})$ and
\[
\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_1=\frac{d}{2\varphi(1-\nu)},\qquad
\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_0=\frac{d}{2\varphi s(1-\nu)} .
\]

\paragraph{Distance.} Write $b^{\star}=(2\varphi(1-\nu))^{-1}$ and parametrise
$\kappa=\kappa^{\star}(1+w\epsilon)$ with $\kappa^{\star}=\varphi(1-\nu)$, so
that $(2\kappa)^{-1}=b^{\star}(1-w\epsilon)+O(\epsilon^2)$. Using
\eqref{eq:distance} with $x_0$ deterministic and
$\E[x_1]=s+\nu\beta_0$, $\E[x_1^2]=s^2+2s\nu\beta_0+\nu\beta_0^2$ at leading
order, the squared distance in units of $\|d\|^{2}$ is
\[
\bigl(\alpha_0+\beta_0-\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_0\bigr)^{2}
+\alpha_1^{2}\E[x_1^{2}]+2\alpha_1(\beta_1-\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_1)\E[x_1]
+(\beta_1-\beta^{\mathrm{eq}}_1)^{2}.
\]
Expanding and retaining the $\epsilon^{2}$ coefficient gives a quadratic in $w$.
Minimising over $w$ and simplifying yields
\[
\min_{w}\;\frac{\|\pir-\pieq\|^{2}}{\|d\|^{2}\epsilon^{2}}
=\frac{(2-\nu)(1+\nu)}{4\varphi^{2}(1-\nu)^{2}},
\]
and taking the square root gives the constant of Theorem~\ref{thm:t2}. The
algebra was carried out with a computer algebra system and the resulting
expression checked against direct numerical minimisation on six markets,
agreeing to $0.11\%$ worst case with fitted exponent $1.0000$.

\begin{remark}
The step that does not generalise mechanically to $T\ge3$ is the last: the
number of terms in the $\epsilon^{2}$ coefficient grows with the horizon, and
while $w^{\star}$ remains the minimiser of a quadratic, we have not found a
closed form for the resulting expression.
\end{remark}
